\chapter{Elastic Waves}

\section[Waves in an Elastic Medium]{Longitudinal and Transverse Waves in an Elastic Medium}
\label{sec-30.1} 

\subsection{}\label{30.1.1} A body is said to be \redem{elastic} and its deformation, due to the application of external forces, is \redem{elastic deformation} if it completely disappears after the external forces are removed. According to \redem{Hooke's law} elastic deformation is directly proportional to the external effects that cause it, i.e. it has a linear dependence on these effects. At sufficiently small deformation, all bodies can he assumed, practically, to be elastic.

The elastic properties of bodies depend upon the nature of the thermal
motion of their molecules and their forces of interaction. For
example, a gaseous body changes its shape without hindrance to suit
the shape of the vessel it is contained in; gas has no \redem{elasticity of
shape}. But a gas has \redem{elasticity of volume}, i.e. the capacity to resist
changes in its volume. This property of a gas is due to the thermal
motion of its molecules and is manifested in the change in the
pressure $p$ of the gas when its volume $V$ is changed.

According to \redem{Hooke's law for volume deformation}, the change
$\dd p$ in the pressure of a gas for a small change $\dd V$ in its
volume is directly proportional to the relative volume defor­ mation,
called the volume strain. Thus
\begin{equation*}%
\dd p = -K \frac{\dd V}{V},
\end{equation*}
where $K$ is called the \redem{bulk modulus of elasticity} of the gas. For an ideal gas \ssect{8.4.1} the value of $K$ depends upon the kind of thermodynamic process of compression \ssect{or expansion} being carried out. At a very slow change in the volume of the gas, the process can be considered isothermal \ssect{8.3.7}, whereas at a rapid change it can be considered adiabatic \ssect{8.3.7}. In the former case $K= p$ and in the latter $K = \varkappa p$, where $\varkappa$ is the adiabatic exponent \ssect{9.5.11}.

\subsection{}\label{30.1.2} The elasticity of a crystalline solid is due to the forces of mutual attraction and repulsion of its particles (ions, atoms or molecules) that make up the body and that have random thermal vibration about the points of its crystal lattice. The forces of interaction of the particle impede deformation of the crystal lattice that is associated with both the volume and shape of the body. Therefore, in addition to their bulk elasticity, solids also have elasticity of form, which is manifested by their resistance to shear deformation \ssect{40.3.9}.

The elasticity of liquids is also due to the forces of intermolecular interaction. But, owing to the fact that the average time $\tau$ that molecules of a liquid remain in a temporarily settled state \ssect{13.2.4} is very short, liquids, like gases, possess only bulk elasticity. They manifest elasticity of form only with respect to variable deformation of ultrahigh frequency with a period that is less than or of the order of $\tau$.

\subsection{}\label{30.1.3} \redem{Elastic waves} are \redem{mechanical disturbances} (deformations) propagated in an elastic medium. Bodies that act on the medium and cause these disturbances are called sources of the waves. A member of
the audience in a theatre, for instance hears the speech and singing of the actors and the sounds of the musical instruments due to the variations in the pressure of the air, reaching the audience and caused by the sources of sound. 

\redem{Sonic}, or \redem{acoustic, waves} are elastic waves of low intensity, i.e. weak mechanical disturbances propagated in an elastic medium.

Sonic waves, acting on the organs of hearing of people, are capable of producing auditory sensations if the frequency $\nu$ of their
corresponding vibrations lies in the range from 16 to \SI{2d4}{\hertz} (\redem{audible sound}). Elastic waves with a frequency $nu < \SI{16}{\hertz}$ are said to be \redem{infrasonic}, whereas those with a frequency $\nu > \SI{2d4}{\hertz}$ are \redem{ultrasonic} (elastic waves) with $\nu \gg \SI{d9}{\hertz}$ are often said to be \redem{hypersonic}.

\subsection{}\label{30.1.4} The propagation of elastic waves in a
medium does not involve the transport of matter.\sidenote{A certain transport of matter (mass transfer) may occur in the propagation in a medium of strong disturbances (for example, shock waves in explosions), when the vibration of the particles of the medium becomes nonlinear.} In a boundless medium, it consists in exciting forced vibrations of parts of the medium that are farther and farther away from the source of the waves. It is feasible here to digress from the discrete (molecular) structure of the medium, dealing with it as a \redem{continuous medium}, distributed continuously in space and possessing definite elastic properties. 

A \redem{particle} of such a medium, having forced vibration, is understood to be a small element of its volume whose size, however, is many times greater than the intermolecular distances so that it contains an enormous number of molecules. Practically, such a particle can be dealt with as a point because even in a gas the intermolecular distances are extremely small (of the order of \SI{d-8}{\meter} under standard conditions).

\subsection{}\label{30.1.5} An elastic wave is said to be \redem{longitudinal} if the particles of the medium vibrate in the direction of wave propagation. Longitudinal waves are associated with bulk deformation of the elastic medium and can therefore propagate in any kind of medium: solid, liquid or gaseous. An example of such waves are the sonic waves in air.

An elastic wave is said to be \redem{transverse} if the particles of the medium vibrate, remaining in planes perpendicular to the direction of wave propagation. Transverse waves are associated with shear deformation of the elastic medium, and, consequently, can be generated and propagated only in media having elasticity of shape, i.e. in solids. An example of transverse waves are
those propagated along the strings of musical instruments. 

Special properties are found in \redem{surface} waves, which propagate along the free surfaces of liquids (or the interface between two immiscible liquids), being a perturbance of the surface due to external effects (dropping of bodies into the liquid, the motion of ships, the wind, etc.). The forces of gravity and surface tension play a decisive role in the generation and propagation of these waves. In surface waves the particles of liquid have transverse and longitudinal vibrations simultaneously, describing elliptical or more complex paths.

\subsection{}\label{30.1.6} A medium is said to be \redem{uniform} if its physical properties, essential in the problem being considered, do not change from point to point. A medium that is uniform with respect to certain physical properties may be non-uniform with respect to others. A mono-crystalline body, for example, is uniform with respect to its elastic properties and, at the same time, optically non-uniform for X-rays.

A medium is \redem{isotropic} if its physical properties that are essential in the problem being considered are the same in all directions. A medium that is isotropic with respect to certain physical properties may be anisotropic with respect to others. Cubic crystals, for instance, are optically isotropic, but are anisotropic with respect to other properties. Gases and liquids are isotropic with respect to any physical properties.

\subsection{}\label{30.1.7} A medium is said to be \redem{linear} if there is a directly proportional relation between the quantities characterizing the external effects being considered that act on the medium and the changes they lead to in the state of the medium. An elastic medium, for example, obeying Hooke’s law \ssect{30.1.1} is linear with respect to its mechanical properties. A dielectric is a linear medium with respect to its electrical properties if its dielectric constant \ssect{14.2.4} is independent of the electric field strength. By analogy, a magnetic material is a linear medium with respect to its magnetic properties if its relative magnetic permeability \ssect{26.4.5} is independent of the magnetic induction of the field.



\section{Travelling Wave Equation}
\label{sec-30.2} 


\subsection{}\label{30.2.1} \redem{Travelling waves} are ones which, in contrast to standing waves \ssect{30.5.4}, transport energy from one part of space to another.

The \redem{travelling wave equation} is the dependence on the coordinates and time of the scalar or vector quantities that characterize the vibration of the medium as the wave being considered is propagated through it. For waves in a solid medium, for instance, such a quantity may be the vector of displacement of the particles of the medium \ssect{30.1.4} from their equilibrium position or the three projections of the vector on the coordinate axes. Longitudinal waves in a gas or liquid are usually characterized by the surplus pressure of the vibrating medium, equal to the difference between its variable and equilibrium pressures.

A \redem{ray} is a curve whose tangent at each point coincides with the direction of propagation of a wave, i.e. with the direction of energy transport by the wave \ssect{30.3.5}. In a uniform medium \ssect{30.1.6} rays are straight lines.

\subsection{}\label{30.2.2} An elastic wave is said to be \redem{sinusoidal}, or \redem{harmonic}, if the corresponding vibration of particles of the medium is harmonic \ssect{28.1.3}. The frequency of this vibration is called the \redem{wave frequency}. Variations in pressure in a gaseous or liquid medium through which a sinusoidal wave propagates also take place according to a harmonic law at a frequency equal to the wave frequency. In a transverse sinusoidal wave, particles of the medium can simultaneously vibrate with harmonic motion at the wave frequency in two mutually perpendicular directions, each of which is perpendicular to the direction of wave propagation. Depending upon the kind of polarization of the resultant vibration \ssect{28.4.9}, distinction is made between the following \redem{types of polarization of transverse sinusoidal waves: elliptical, circular and linear (or plane)}.

\subsection{}\label{30.2.3} A mechanical disturbance (deformation) propagates in an elastic medium at the finite velocity $v$. Therefore, disturbances, initiated by the wave source at the instant $t_{0}$ of time, reach an arbitrary point $M$ of the medium at the instant $t - t_{0}$. The greater the path $l$ that the wave travels from its source to point $M$, the greater the difference $t - t_{0} = l/v$. Consequently, the vibration at point $M$ lags in phase behind that at the source of the waves.

The \redem{wave surface}, or \redem{wavefront}, is the locus of all the points at which the phase of the vibration has the same value at the instant of time being considered. The difference $t - t_{0}$ is the same for all points in a wavefront. Through each point of a medium subject to wave motion one wavefront can be passed that corresponds to the value of the phase of the vibration at this point at the instant of time being considered. Many different values of the phase of a vibration correspond to a family of wavefronts. In a uniform isotropic medium \ssect{30.1.6} the wavefronts are orthogonal to the rays \ssect{30.2.1}.

\subsection{}\label{30.2.4} A wave is said to be \redem{plane} if its wavefronts constitute a system of parallel planes. In a plane wave, propagating along the $OX$ axis, all values of $s$, characterizing the vibratory motion of the medium, depend only upon the time $t$ and the coordinate $x$ of the point $M$ being considered in the medium. If there is no absorption of waves in the medium \ssect{30.3.7}, the vibration at point $M$ differs from that at the origin of coordinates $O$ only in that it is shifted in time by the amount $x/v$, where $v$ is the wave velocity. Hence, in a plane wave, propagating along the \redem{positive} direction of the $OX$ axis, $s$ is a function of the difference $(t - x/v)$ and the \redem{equation} of such a \redem{plane} wave is:
\begin{equation*}%
s = f \, \left( t - \frac{x}{v} \right).
\end{equation*}
Accordingly, the equation for a plane wave propagating in the opposite direction is
\begin{equation*}%
s = f \, \left( t + \frac{x}{v} \right).
\end{equation*}

\subsection{}\label{30.2.5} The \redem{equation of a plane sinusoidal}, or simply \redem{sine, wave}, propagating in a non-absorbing medium along the positive direction of the $OX$ axis, is
\begin{align*}%
s & = A \sin \left[ \omega \left( t - \frac{x}{v} \right) + \varphi_{0} \right] \\
& =  A \sin \left(  \omega t - \frac{ \omega}{v}x  + \varphi_{0} \right) \qor \\
& =  A \sin \left(  \frac{2\pi}{T} t - \frac{2 \pi}{vT}x  + \varphi_{0} \right),
\end{align*}
where $A = \text{const.}$ is the amplitude of vibration and is called the \redem{wave amplitude}, $\omega = 2pi/T$ is the angular frequency of the wave, $T$ is the period of vibration, and $\varphi_{0}$ is the initial phase of the vibration (at the instant of time $t = 0$) in points of the coordinate plane $x = 0$. The quantity $\Phi = \omega t - \omega_{x}/v + \varphi_{0}$, equal to the phase of the vibration at an arbitrary point with the coordinate $x$, is called the \redem{phase of the plane wave} (see also \ssect{30.2.6}). 

\subsection{}\label{30.2.6} The distance $\lambda = vT$ that a sine wave travels during the time equal to the period of vibration is called the \redem{wavelength}. The wavelength is equal to the distance between two closest points in a medium in which the phase difference of the vibration equals $2\pi$.

Along with the wavelength, use is made of another characteris­ tic of a sine wave. It is the \redem{wavenumber}
\begin{equation*}%
k =  \frac{2 \pi}{\lambda} = \frac{2 \pi}{vT} = \frac{\omega}{v}. 
\end{equation*}
Hence, the equation of a plane sine wave \ssect{30.2.5} can be written in the form
\begin{align*}%
s & = A \sin \left(  \omega t - \frac{2 \pi}{\lambda} x  + \varphi_{0} \right) \\
& = A \sin \left(  \omega t - k x  + \varphi_{0} \right).
\end{align*}
Accordingly, the phase of this plane wave is $\Phi = \omega t - kx + \varphi_{0}$. 

\subsection{}\label{30.2.7} The \redem{wave vector} is the vector $\vb{k}$, equal in magnitude to the \redem{wavenumber} $k$ and having the direction along the ray \ssect{30.2.1} at the point $M$ being considered in the medium. The wave vector of a plane sine wave does not depend on the choice of point $M$, and the equation for such a wave can be written in the form
\begin{equation*}%
s =  A \sin \left(  \omega t - \vb{k}\vb{r}  + \alpha \right),
\end{equation*}
where $\vb{r}$ is the radius vector of point $M$, and $\alpha$ is the initial phase of vibration at the origin of coordinates, i.e. at the point $\vb{r} = 0$.

On the basis of the Euler formula \ssect{28.1.7}, the equation of a plane sine wave can be written in exponential form, which is convenient for differentiation:
\begin{equation*}%
\tilde{s} =  A \exp  i \left(  \omega t - \vb{k}\vb{r}  + \delta \right),
\end{equation*}
where $\delta = \alpha - \pi/2$. Only the real part of the complex quantity $\tilde{s}$ is of physical meaning, i.e. the quantity $s = \Re{\tilde{s}}$. Therefore, in making use of $\tilde{s}$ to determine some characteristic of a wave, it is necessary to discard the imaginary part of the final complex expression.

\subsection{}\label{30.2.8} A wave is said to be \redem{spherical} if its wavefronts are con­ centric spheres. The centre of these spheres is called the \redem{wave centre}. Such waves are generated in a uniform isotropic medium by a solitary point source. The \redem{equation of a diverging spherical wave} is of the form
\begin{equation*}%
\frac{1}{r} \, f \, \left( t - \frac{r}{v}\right),
\end{equation*}
where $r$ is the distance from the wave centre to the point $M$ being considered in the medium, and $v$ is the wave velocity.

In the case of a \redem{spherical sine wave}
\begin{equation*}%
\frac{a_{0}}{r} \, \sin \left( \omega t - k \vb{r} + \alpha \right),
\end{equation*}
where $ a_{0}/ = A (r)$ is the wave amplitude \ssect{30.3.6}, $a_{0}$ is a physical quantity, numerically equal to the wave amplitude at unit distance from the wave centre, $\alpha$ is the initial phase of vibration at the wave centre, and $\Phi = \omega t  - kr + \alpha$ is the phase of the spherical wave.

In the exponential form, the equation of a spherical sine wave is
\begin{equation*}%
s = \frac{a_{0}}{r} \exp i \left( \omega t - \vb{k}\vb{r} + \delta \right) = \frac{a_{0}}{r} \exp i \left( \omega t - kr + \delta \right),
\end{equation*}
where $\delta = \alpha - \pi/2$, $\vb{r}$ is the radius vector from the wave centre to the point $M$ being considered, and wave vector $\vb{k}$ at point $M$ is in the direction radially away from the wave centre.

Real sources of waves are always of finite size. But they can be dealt with as point sources and the waves they generate in a uniform isotropic medium as spherical ones if the distance $r$ from the source to the points being considered in the medium is considerably greater than the dimensions of the source. When $r$ is extremely great, any small portions of a wavefront can be regarded as being plane surfaces.

\subsection{}\label{30.2.9} The propagation of waves in a uniform isotropic medium can be described by a partial differential equation, called the \redem{wave equation}. Thus
\begin{align*}%
\pdv[2]{s}{x} + \pdv[2]{s}{y} + \pdv[2]{s}{z} & = \frac{1}{v^{2}} \, \pdv[2]{s}{t}, \qor \\   
\Delta s & = \frac{1}{v^{2}} \, \pdv[2]{s}{t}.
\end{align*}
Here $s$ is a physical quantity that characterizes a disturbance propagating in the medium at the velocity $v$, 
\begin{equation*}%
\Delta = \pdv[2]{x} + \pdv[2]{y} + \pdv[2]{z},
\end{equation*}
is the Laplace operator, or Laplacian. This equation is satisfied, among others, by a plane wave \ssect{30.2.7} and a diverging spherical wave \ssect{30.2.8}.

Function $s$, characterizing a \redem{sine wave} in a uniform isotropic medium, satisfies two partial differential equations at the same time:
\begin{equation*}%
\Delta s = -k^{2}s,
\end{equation*}
where $k$ is the wavenumber \ssect{30.2.6}, and
\begin{equation*}%
\pdv[2]{s}{t} = -k^{2}s,
\end{equation*}
where co is the angular frequency of the wave.

\subsection{}\label{30.2.10} The velocity $v$ of propagation of a \redem{sine wave} is called the \redem{phase velocity}. It is equal to the velocity of displacement in space of the points in a surface that corresponds to any fixed value of the phase of a sine wave. For example, in the case of a plane sine wave \ssect{30.2.6} it follows from the condition $\omega t - kx + \varphi_{0} = \text{const.}$ that $\dv*{x}{t} = \omega/k = v$. In the same way, in the case of a spherical sine wave \ssect{30.2.8}, it follows from the condition $\omega t - kr + \alpha = \text{const.}$ that $\dv*{r}{t} = \omega / k = v$.

\section{Phase Velocity and Energy of Elastic Waves}
\label{sec-30.3} 


\subsection{}\label{30.3.1} The \redem{phase velocity of sonic waves (velocity of sound) in a liquid or gas} is
\begin{equation*}%
v = \sqrt{\frac{K}{\rho}},
\end{equation*}
where $\rho$ is the density of the undisturbed medium, and $K$ is the bulk modulus of elasticity of the medium \ssect{30.1.1}. The frequency of audible sonic waves $\nu > \SI{16}{\hertz}$, and the process of deforming the medium can be assumed to be adiabatic \ssect{8.3.7}, i.e. $K = -V (\dv*{p}{V})_{ad}$. The relation between the pressure $p$ and volume $V$ for an ideal gas \ssect{8.4.1} in an adiabatic process is: $pV^{\varkappa} = \text{const.}$, where $\varkappa$ is the adiabatic exponent \ssect{9.5.11}, so that $K = \varkappa p$. Therefore, the \redem{velocity of sound in an ideal gas} is equal to
\begin{equation*}%
v = \sqrt{\frac{\varkappa p}{\rho}} = \sqrt{\frac{\varkappa RT}{\mu}},
\end{equation*}
where $\mu$, is the molar mass of the gas, $T$ is its absolute temperature, and $R$ is the universal gas constant.

\subsection{}\label{30.3.2} The \redem{phase velocity of transverse elastic waves} in a uniform isotropic solid medium is
\begin{equation*}%
v = \sqrt{\frac{G}{\rho}},
\end{equation*}
where $G$ is the shear modulus of the medium \ssect{40.3.9} and $\rho$ is its density.

The propagation of longitudinal waves in a \redem{thin long rod} is associated with its longitudinal tension and compression. Hence, the phase velocity of such waves is
\begin{equation*}%
v = \sqrt{\frac{E}{\rho}},
\end{equation*}
where $E$ is Young’s modulus \ssect{40.3.6} for the rod material.

The \redem{velocity of propagation of transverse waves along a stretched string (a thin flexible cord)} is
\begin{equation*}%
v = \sqrt{\frac{F}{\rho S}},
\end{equation*}
where $F$ is the stretching force (tension) in the string, and $\rho$ and $S$ are the density of the string material and the cross-sectional area of the string.

\subsection{}\label{30.3.3} An elastic medium in which mechanical waves propagate has kinetic energy of vibratory motion of its particles and potential energy due to deformation \ssect{30.1.1}. If $\vb{v}_{1}$ the velocity of particles of the medium, then the \redem{volume kinetic energy density of the medium} is 
\begin{equation*}%
E^{d}_{k} = \dv{E_{k}}{V} = \frac{1}{2} \rho v_{1}^{2},
\end{equation*}
where $\rho$ is the density of the medium, and $\dd E_{k}$ is the kinetic energy of all the particles in the small volume $\dd V$ of the medium. This volume has been selected so that throughout it the velocity $\vb{v}_{1}$ is the same.

The \redem{volume potential energy density of an elastically deformed medium} is
\begin{equation*}%
E^{d}_{p} = \dv{E_{p}}{V} = \frac{1}{2} \rho v^{2} \varepsilon^{2},
\end{equation*}
where $\dd E_{p}$ is the potential energy of a uniformly deformed
small portion of the medium of volume $\dd V$, $v$ is the phase velocity in the medium, and $\varepsilon$ is the relative deformation. 

The \redem{volume energy density of elastic waves} is understood to be the volume density $E^{d}$ of the mechanical energy of a medium due to the propagation of these waves. Thus
\begin{equation*}%
E^{d} = E^{d}_{k} + E^{d}_{p} =  \frac{1}{2} \rho ( v_{1}^{2} + v^{2} \varepsilon^{2}).
\end{equation*}

\subsection{}\label{30.3.4} If a longitudinal plane travelling wave \ssect{30.2.4} propagates in a medium, then $v_{1} = \pdv*{s}{t}$, where $s$ is the displacement of the particles, and $\varepsilon = \pdv*{s}{x} = - v_{1}/v$, so that
\begin{equation*}%
E^{d}_{p} = E^{d}_{k} \qand E^{d} = \rho v_{1}^{2} = \rho \left( \pdv{s}{t}\right)^{2}.
\end{equation*}
At each point of the medium subject to wave motion, $E^{d}_{k}$ and
$E^{d}_{p} $ are \redem{alike} functions of time. Consequently, $E^{d}$ also varies with time. This principle is valid for any travelling waves in an elastic medium, regardless of the shape of their wavefronts and the type of deformation of the medium. It follows from the law of conservation of energy as applied to the process of the propagation of vibrations in an elastic medium. 

To set regions of the medium more and more distant from the wave source into vibratory motion it is necessary to expend energy, which is supplied to the medium by the source. Consequently, the propagation of elastic waves is inseparably associated with the transport of energy from certain parts of the medium to others. This is precisely why the volume energy density $E^{d}$ of the waves depends both on the coordinates and on the time as well.

For a plane travelling sine wave in a non-absorbing medium \ssect{30.2.5}
\begin{align*}%
E^{d} & = \rho A^{2} \omega^{2} \cos^{2} (\omega t - kx + \varphi_{0}) \\
& = \frac{1}{2} \rho A^{2}\omega^{2} [1 + \cos 2(\omega t - kx + \varphi_{0})],
\end{align*}
where $A = \text{const.}$ is the wave amplitude.

In the case of a diverging spherical sine wave in a non-absorbing medium \ssect{30.2.8}
\begin{equation*}%
E^{d} = \rho A^{2}\omega^{2}\cos^{2} (\omega t - kr + \alpha),
\end{equation*}
where $A = a_{0}/r$ is the amplitude of the wave.

The average volume energy density over one period is
\begin{equation*}%
E^{d} = \frac{1}{2} \rho A^{2} \omega^{2}.
\end{equation*}

\subsection{}\label{30.4.2} On the basis of the principle of wave superposition and a Fourier expansion (\ssect{28.4.6} and \ssect{28.4.7}) any nonsine wave can be replaced by an equivalent system of sine waves, i.e. it can be represented in the form of a group of waves, called a wave train or packet. The totality of frequency values for these sine waves is called the frequency spectrum (or simply spectrum) of the nonsine wave. In accordance with the nature of the oscillation excited by the wave, the wave’s frequency spectrum can be either discrete \ssect{28.4.6} or continuous \ssect{28.4.7}.

The law of propagation of an arbitrary disturbance (signal), which is a nonsine wave, in a linear medium is simple only provided that the medium is nondispersive \ssect{30.3.8}. In this case the signal travels in the medium without changing its “shape” because all the sine waves making up the wave train have the same phase velocity, which is equal to the velocity of the signal.

In a dispersive medium, the sine components of the wave train, corresponding to the nonsine wave, propagate at different
velocities. Therefore, this wave train “spreads”as it propagates, thereby changing the “shape”of the signal. \subsection{}\label{30.5.3} When waves interfere, their energies cannot be simply added together. At interference maxima, the intensity of the resultant wave is greater than the sum of the intensities of the superposed waves, and at minima, it is less than their sum. Wave interference leads to a redistribution of vibrational energy between neighbouring regions of the medium. But on an average, for a large region of space, the energy of the resultant wave is equal to the sum of the energies of the interfering waves. This follows from the laws of conservation and conversion of energy.
